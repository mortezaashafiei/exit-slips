01 · About

Soil that turns from sand to clay

I am a Ph.D. candidate in geotechnical engineering at the University of Massachusetts Amherst and a student researcher with ARROW, the Academic Center for Reliability & Resilience of Offshore Wind. My work focuses on glauconite sand, which is common along the U.S. Atlantic continental shelf in areas of offshore wind development. Glauconite grains are soft (Mohs hardness of about 2) and crush easily, so this “greensand” can change from sand-like to clay-like behavior, a risk for both foundation installation and long-term performance.

I study how glauconite behaves under static and cyclic loading, and I am developing laboratory methods that mimic foundation installation, so we can determine how crushing changes soil properties and how much the sand degrades, using both laboratory and in-situ testing. Better predictions of these properties can reduce extensive and costly testing and the chance of pile-driving refusal offshore, where remediation can cost orders of magnitude more than on land.

Before UMass, I earned my M.Sc. at Amirkabir University of Technology (Tehran Polytechnic), where I designed and operated a large consolidation chamber to measure how piles in clay gain capacity over time. Today I also teach undergraduate engineering mechanics and geotechnical courses and work with undergraduate researchers in the laboratory.

Ph.D. research · offshore wind

How glauconite sand degrades

Glauconite sands from the Hornerstown and Navesink formations (New Jersey) are degraded in the laboratory with methods matched to the stress regimes around a driven pile: one-dimensional compression, ball milling, and dispersion-cup mixing. I track the changes in grain size and plasticity, and how far each method breaks the particles down.

Key insightGrain size alone cannot classify degraded glauconite. How the fines are produced, through stress regime and free water, controls whether the soil becomes plastic.

Offshore site investigation

Reducing CPTu thrust in glauconite sand

Crushable glauconite combines high cone resistance with high rod friction, which can stop a piezocone (CPTu) early. In a field program at a New Jersey site in two glauconite formations, part of the Piling in Glauconitic Sand (PIGS) Joint Industry Project, we compared larger cones, friction reducers of different length and diameter, multiple reducers, and water jetting.

Key resultA larger 15 cm² cone, a larger-diameter reducer, and upper-bevel water jetting significantly reduced thrust; longer or multiple standard reducers did not.

Deep foundations · M.Sc. research

Pile setup in clay, measured in a calibration chamber

In a 400 mm × 1000 mm consolidation chamber, an instrumented CPT-size model pile (three strain gauges and three pore-pressure transducers) was driven or jacked into kaolinite clay at OCR 1.2 and 4.8, then load tested from 1 hour to 90 days.

Key resultJacked-in piles started with higher shaft friction, but driven piles set up faster and ended higher in the long term, attributed to greater soil disturbance during driving.

Particle morphology

3D particle shape from 2D images

A flat grain resting on its largest face looks round from above, so 2D images overestimate sphericity. Using a sequential sieving test, run like a normal sieve test but stopped at several time steps, together with X-ray micro-CT of more than 3,000 particles per material, we linked 2D shape indices to true 3D sphericity.

Key resultThe initial passing rate correlates strongly with 3D sphericity, and grading curves from 3D imaging agree with sieve analysis, while 2D ones do not without correction.

Journal articles

  1. 2026 Journal article

    Mitigation of CPT thrust force through glauconite sand

    Westgate, Z. J., Senanayake, A., Shafiei, M., & DeGroot, D. J.

    Canadian Geotechnical Journal, 63, 1–8 · doi:10.1139/cgj-2026-0117

    • Field CPTu
    • 2 glauconite formations, NJ
    • Thrust-reduction methods
    Publisher (DOI) ResearchGate
    Summary

    Glauconite sands crush easily under shear and compression, producing high piezocone (CPTu) penetration forces from a combination of high tip resistance and high rod friction. Routine fixes, such as larger cones and friction reducers, can still lead to early refusal. A CPTu field investigation at a New Jersey test site in two glauconite formations compared larger cones, standard friction reducers, longer reducers, larger-diameter reducers, multiple reducers spaced along the rod, and water jetting with a standard reducer. A larger 15 cm² cone, a larger-diameter reducer on a 10 cm² cone, and water jetting from the upper bevel of a 10 cm² cone significantly reduced thrust and allowed greater penetration, although effectiveness was layer-specific; longer or multiple standard reducers gave no improvement.

  2. 2024 Journal article

    Quantifying three-dimensional sphericity indices of irregular fine particles from 2D images through sequential sieving tests

    Kaviani-Hamedani, F., Esmailzade, M., Adineh, K., Shafiei, M., & Shirkavand, D.

    Granular Matter, 26(1), Article 13 · doi:10.1007/s10035-023-01376-1

    • X-ray micro-CT, 3,000+ particles/material
    • 7 natural + 7 synthetic materials
    • Zingg flatness & elongation
    Publisher (DOI) ResearchGate
    Summary

    Proposes a way to predict 3D sphericity from ordinary 2D image analysis using a new sieving procedure. The 3D sphericity of grains from seven irregular granular materials (more than 3,000 particles each) was measured from X-ray micro-CT slices and compared with 2D indices from SEM image processing; seven semi-regular synthetic materials extended the range of shapes. In the sequential sieving test, run like a conventional sieve test but at several time steps, the initial passing rate correlated strongly with 3D sphericity. The study gives correlations between 2D and 3D sphericity, two equations for the most frequent Zingg flatness and elongation ratios, and a correction that brings 2D grading curves into line with 3D imaging and sieve analysis.

  3. 2023 Journal article

    Investigation of soil setup effects on pile response in clay considering overconsolidation ratio and installation method through physical modeling

    Fakharian, K., Shafiei, M., & Hafezan, S.

    Canadian Geotechnical Journal, 60(4), 488–503 · doi:10.1139/cgj-2021-0686

    • Kaolinite, OCR 1.2 & 4.8
    • Driven vs. jacked-in
    • Load tests 1 h – 90 d
    Publisher (DOI)
    Summary

    Investigates how soil setup changes the axial resistance and stiffness of small-scale piles in clay, focusing on overconsolidation ratio and installation method. In a purpose-built consolidation chamber, an instrumented model pile was driven or jacked into saturated kaolinite at OCR 1.2 (NC) and 4.8 (OC) and load tested from 1 hour to 90 days. Installation induced higher pore pressures in NC clay, and OC clay gave higher shaft friction both at the end of installation and after setup. Jacked-in piles had higher friction at the end of installation, but driven piles, with their higher setup rate, reached greater long-term friction, attributed to more soil disturbance during driving. The contributions of excess pore-pressure dissipation and non-pore-pressure components were separated, and a relation was proposed for the change in pile stiffness over time.

Conference papers & presentations

  1. 2027 Conference paper · submitted

    Effect of particle degradation mechanisms on glauconite sand behavior

    Shafiei, M., Jaffé, D., Westgate, Z. J., & DeGroot, D. J.

    ASCE Geo-Congress 2027 · abstract accepted, full paper submitted

    • 1D compression · ball milling · dispersion cup
    • Relative breakage index
    • Plasticity of degraded fines

    Glauconite sands degrade easily, which changes how they classify and behave in site characterization, pile–soil interaction, and tunnel boring. Two natural glauconite sands were degraded with three laboratory methods matched to the stress fields around a driven pile. Dispersion-cup mixing and ball milling degraded the soils extensively, while one-dimensional compression to 50 MPa produced only small changes. Dispersion-cup mixing released more clay minerals and raised plasticity; ball milling released mostly non-plastic clay-size particles, with negligible or even lower plasticity for a similar gain in fines. Particle-size classification alone is therefore insufficient: laboratory degradation should reflect the stress regime and free-water conditions of the field application.
